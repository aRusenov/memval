## Continual retention
The cornerstone of any memory system is the ability to retain important information in the face of ongoing environmental pressure. The fine balance between incorporating new memories while retaining existing ones is the stability-plasticity dilemma (Kudithipudi et al., 2022; Bidaki et al., 2025), and it is what makes continual retention non-trivial to evaluate, as more retention is not necessarily better. Memory is a finite resource: a fixed neural substrate can hold only so many traces before new memories begin to degrade old ones (Benna & Fusi, 2016). Biology thus employs strategies that all discard: attention-gated rapid encoding, gradual consolidation, generalization (McClelland et al., 2020), reconstructive recall from compressed representations (Nagy et al., 2020). Forgetting under such a regime is thus functional rather than a failure (Richards and Frankland, 2017). The ideal balance between stability and plasticity in continual retention is beyond the scope of this benchmark. Instead, we evaluate both sides using different metrics to highlight where a candidate memory system holds and where it breaks down.
In the symbolic suite we report a retention matrix, which contains the recall score of every sequence, re-measured after each newly learned one. Read across a row it scores stability, reflecting resistance to interference from newly acquired sequences, and the fraction retained at each interposition distance gives the shape of the loss rather than its magnitude alone. Read down its diagonal it scores the opposite quantity: how well each sequence was acquired at the moment it was presented, with everything earlier already occupying the substrate. Reporting both is necessary rather than thorough, because every measure of stability is trivially maximised by a system that stops encoding, and we therefore also combine the two into a single index that falls whenever either of them does. We pair this with two protocols in which a learned association is invalidated and abandoning it is the correct behaviour rather than a failure: a reversal in the spatial suite, where a place-reward contingency is flipped (Fig. 2), and an AB/AC transfer design in the symbolic suite, where the same cues are re-paired with new targets. Each is run against a matched control in which an equal quantity of new material is learned without competing for anything already stored, so that targeted overwriting is separable from generic interference. Finally, because a finite substrate must discard, we ask not only how much survives but whether what survives is what remains in use: part of the learned material is re-presented and the rest is not, and we score the difference between how the two fare. 

## One-shot learning
Living organisms possess the remarkable ability to learn from brief, single encounters (Tulving, 2002). In contrast to machine learning pipelines that revisit each example many times, animals often need no more than a single exposure to remember. For instance, rodents navigating a maze return to a water source discovered only once (Rosenberg et al., 2021), and a putative neural mechanism for this fast-encoding, one-shot regime has been identified in the hippocampus: a single dendritic plateau potential can induce a new hippocampal place field in a single trial, potentiating inputs in a seconds-long window around it (behavioral time-scale synaptic plasticity; Bittner et al., 2017). One-shot learning thus sits on the plastic extreme of the stability-plasticity continuum, rapidly creating a memory trace that is still labile until consolidation. The brain does not consistently operate in this regime, but instead gates it with novelty, salience, and attention, transiently raising hippocampal plasticity through neuromodulation (ref). For there to be balance, memory must support gradual learning, along with rapid learning for salient, contextually important experiences. This division of regimes is the core proposal of complementary learning systems theory: the hippocampus encodes quickly, using sparse, minimally overlapping codes so that rapid storage does not overwrite what it already holds. The neocortex, on the other hand, learns slowly, extracting structure across experiences, with hippocampal replay providing the interleaving that lets a once-experienced episode be integrated gradually rather than catastrophically (McClelland et al., 1995). More recent revisits of this account have suggested that the neocortex is also capable of fast acquisition when the new experiences are compatible with existing schemas (McClelland et al., 2020; Kumaran et al., 2016). The speed of integration is therefore not fixed by the system doing the encoding, but modulated by how well the new item fits what is already known. 
We evaluate one-shot learning as a function of the number of exposures, from a single presentation upward. One-shot acquisition is not difficult to achieve in itself, as a model which indiscriminately overwrites is one-shot by construction. We thus pair recall score from exposure with the retention matrix measure of previous sequences. We further explore the schema-consistency axis and ask whether consistency with prior structure influences the speed of sequence acquisition. 

## Serial order
Perhaps the most defining characteristic of sequence memory is that it keeps not only what happened but also when, relative to everything else within an episode. Representing time thus becomes a matter of preserving order and the temporal distance between items. For order, the behavioral signature is the temporal contiguity effect: recall of an item biases towards recalling its temporal neighbours, and this effect robustly appears across decades of research on episodic recall (Kahana, 2020). One candidate neural mechanism is theta-scale sequence compression: theta oscillations compress seconds-long segments of experience into single ~100-millisecond theta cycles, preserving within each cycle the order in which the events were experienced. Compression leaves adjacent events separated by mere tens of milliseconds, inside the window of spike-timing-dependent plasticity, whose asymmetry strengthens forward associations over backward ones (Buzsáki & Tingley, 2018). For the interval, hippocampal time cells encode the temporal gap between two successive events, and re-time when the trained gap is lengthened (MacDonald et al., 2011). <<add few words for autonomous recall?>>
We evaluate serial order on both axes separately, but primarily focus on the failures of order: order never established, items recalled, but out of sequence, associations present but autonomous unrolling collapses. For models that support an online regime and state of time, we probe whether the interval separating two items is retained. 

## Pattern completion
Part of what makes memory adaptive is that it not only stores experiences, but it also allows their recall from a fragment. Suppose an animal encounters a lion next to a water hole. In the future, it shouldn’t need the exact full arrangement of the encounter to re-experience the event. Instead, only seeing the water hole or a lion-like silhouette should serve as a recall cue to retrieve the entire episode. Incomplete cues can thus be viewed in two independent ways. The first is structural and relates to what part of the original experience is presented. The second is corruption: noise enters at every stage of the path from the sensory environment to the trace itself, from the thermodynamic noise of molecules arriving at the receptors to the unreliable synaptic transmission as the signal propagates inward (Faisal et al., 2008). In order for memory to be useful under these conditions, it must be able to recover the stored whole from both partial and corrupted cues. 
Behaviorally, the case for pattern completion from an incomplete cue is well-established. When people study events made of several elements and are cued with one of them, the remaining elements return together rather than independently (Horner et al., 2015), and recognition falls off in graded fashion as progressively less of a learned image is shown (Vieweg et al., 2019). Furthermore, the extent of holistic retrieval has been shown to covary with activity in the hippocampal CA3 subfield (Grande et al., 2019). Mechanistically, the attractor account attributes this to auto-association in CA3 (Kumaran et al., 2016; Neunuebel & Knierim, 2014), where plasticity among the features co-active at a single moment of experience binds them into one stored pattern, so that a fragment presented later drives the remainder of that same pattern back into activity.
Noise in the cue itself is harder to study in a behaving animal, as arena landmarks can be removed or displaced, but parametrically quantifiable noise is hard to add to the environment. Evidence comes instead from virtual reality, where graded visual noise (virtual fog) was introduced into a familiar arena while place cells were recorded from CA1 and CA3 (Shin et al., 2022). CA3 cells largely retained their fields and responded mainly with rate changes, whereas part of the CA1 population remapped globally, indicating pattern completion–like stability specific to CA3. Taken together, both lines of evidence support a CA3 role in pattern completion with resistance to incomplete or corrupted cues.
We evaluate this capacity by manipulating both aspects of the cue separately while probing cued recall and autoregressive rollout. For the corrupted cue, we increasingly inject cue noise, whereas for the partial cue, completeness is manipulated by masking a swept fraction of the cue's dimensions. Notably, for the autoregressive protocol we only manipulate the initial cue, which additionally allows us to score how an initial error propagates. While it’s common evaluation practice to also perturb network activity with random noise, here it is beyond the scope due to the heterogeneous nature of the models tested.

Masking a cue is only informative if its dimensions individually mean something, so the partial-cue manipulation is run over a hierarchically generated feature code rather than over unstructured embeddings, whose dimensions are an arbitrary basis in which removing a subset merely rescales similarity to everything at once. In that code each item's features divide into those inherited from its category and those belonging to the item alone, which lets us ask not only how much of a cue is needed but which part: whether losing an item's category context costs the same as losing what distinguishes it from its neighbours. Because a sufficiently masked cue eventually stops picking out its own item at all — several studied items come to share one input — we compute this identifiability directly from the stimulus, before any model is run, and report recall against it. Below that line a failure is a property of the cue rather than of the memory; above it, recall exceeding what the degraded cue specifies on its own is completion in the strict sense, as opposed to cue matching.
	
## Sequence disambiguation
While a stable memory trace should be recoverable from a degraded cue, it should also not be returned when a different episode is being asked for. Discrimination and tolerance are complementary demands for memory, yet they are also transformations in opposite directions: separating similar experiences makes them more distinct, while completion aims to match a partial pattern to a similar stored whole (Kumaran et al., 2016). The ability of a sequence memory to disambiguate between similar, yet distinct episodes clearly deserves its own evaluation.
In the hippocampus, separation is handled at encoding time by the dentate gyrus (DG). Novel patterns from sensory regions or high-cortical areas are orthogonalized by activating only a small subset of DG neurons before projecting downstream to the CA3. This sparsification ensures the new CA3 pattern is distinct from previous activations (Kumaran et al., 2016). When the input is not only similar but identical, hippocampal cells acquire conjunctive item-place responses as the same item carries distinct meaning across different locations (ref). Further, if no distinguishing sensory signal accompanies the input – for instance, when the mouse is navigating a maze stem and needs to decide on the next direction – the distinction is carried by splitter cells that fire differently at the same location according to the trajectory in progress (Duvelle et al., 2023). The latter showcases that the hippocampus keeps track not only of the current online stream of information, but also of recent experiences, and possibly future inferences. << expand on theories of drifting context? >>. Taken together, these findings describe a stack of mechanisms for telling similar experiences apart, each operating with less external support than the last: orthogonalization where inputs merely resemble one another, conjunctive binding where a second stream is concurrently available, and a carried signal where consistent external input is lacking.
We evaluate this capacity with sequences that share a common stretch of observations, while grading how much external support is available through that stretch. Specifically, we vary the provided distinguishing signal, from persistently available throughout the shared stretch to only present in the beginning of the sequence. The latter case is the most complex, as whatever holds the sequence apart from the others must be internal to the model. The length of the shared stretch, the number of episodes sharing it, and the delivery interval are varied independently.
